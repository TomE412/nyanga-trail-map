SkyRun 2026 Athlete Medical Guide

Emergency recognition prevention and field action

A note from the race director

SkyRun is remote, long and exposed. A small problem can become serious before help reaches you. Your first responsibility is to recognise warning signs early, stop before your judgment or mobility deteriorates, and contact the race medical team with an accurate location. Looking after another athlete is part of your race.

This guide supports first aid and good decisions; it does not replace training, professional medical care, the race briefing or the instructions of the medical team. Use only skills and medicines you are trained or authorised to use.

Before the start Save both emergency contacts, write them on waterproof paper, and confirm how to send your tracker identity and GPS position.

If you are unsure

Treat a change in mental state as an emergency. Heatstroke and exercise-associated hyponatraemia can look similar, and giving large volumes of fluid to the wrong patient can make the situation worse. If the athlete is hot and confused, cool immediately, do not force oral fluid, and contact the medical team.

A condition not listed

Stop and call whenever symptoms are severe, unusual, worsening, recurrent or prevent safe movement, or when they affect awareness, breathing or circulation. Use DRSABC, protect the athlete from the environment and describe what you see rather than trying to make a diagnosis.

Emergency response

Start every incident with DRSABC. In a remote race, scene safety, early communication and protection from the environment happen alongside medical care.

DRSABC visual summary. Use the complete response table and CPR instructions below.

Step

What to do

D Danger

Check cliffs, loose rock, traffic, snakes, lightning, cold, heat and moving athletes. Put on gloves. Move the casualty only if the location is unsafe.

R Response

Speak loudly and tap the shoulders. Note whether the athlete is alert, responds to voice, responds to pain, or is unresponsive.

S Send for help

Call Jazzy on +263 78 066 1516 or ACE Air and Ambulance on +263 782 999 901. Send athlete identity, exact GPS position, route feature, symptoms, start time and actions taken.

A Airway

Open the airway with head tilt and chin lift unless a neck injury is suspected. Use a jaw thrust only if trained. Remove visible loose obstructions only.

B Breathing

Check for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping, snoring or irregular agonal breaths are not normal breathing.

C CPR and circulation

If the athlete is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start chest compressions. Control life-threatening bleeding as soon as it is identified.

Collapsed athlete and CPR

1 Activate the race emergency response and send for the AED.

2 Place the athlete on a firm surface. Begin 30 chest compressions at 100 to 120 per minute, 5 to 6 cm deep, allowing full recoil.

3 If trained and willing, give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions. If not, continue uninterrupted chest compressions.

4 Attach the AED as soon as it arrives, follow its prompts and minimise pauses in compressions.

5 Continue until the athlete shows signs of life, trained help takes over, the scene becomes unsafe or you are physically unable to continue.

Record the time of collapse, CPR start, AED analysis and every shock.

While you wait

  • Never leave a sick athlete alone. Keep one person with the athlete and, if the group is large enough, one person responsible for communication.
  • Keep the athlete protected from the ground and weather and continue the first aid already started.
  • Recheck responsiveness and breathing regularly. Report any change to the medical team.
  • Preserve phone or tracker power, remain at the agreed position unless it becomes unsafe, and follow the evacuation instructions you receive.
  • Record the time symptoms began, changes in responsiveness, fluids taken, urine passed, medicines used and every intervention.

Fainting choking and water emergencies

A brief collapse can still have a dangerous cause. Protect the athlete from a fall, check breathing immediately and escalate early when recovery is not rapid and complete.

Fainting and near fainting

Recognise Light-headedness, pallor, sweating, nausea, tunnel vision, weakness or a brief loss of consciousness. Collapse during exertion is more concerning than feeling faint immediately after stopping.

1 Help the athlete sit or lie down before they fall. If there is no trauma and breathing is normal, lie them flat; legs may be raised slightly if this does not cause pain or worsen breathing.

2 Check for heat illness, bleeding, low blood sugar, chest pain, stroke signs, allergy and abnormal breathing. Loosen tight clothing and protect from the environment.

3 Call urgently if symptoms do not improve within 1 to 2 minutes, fainting occurred during exertion, the athlete was injured, or there is chest pain, palpitations, breathlessness, severe headache, confusion or repeated collapse.

4 Do not allow the athlete to stand suddenly or resume the race until the cause has been assessed and normal function has returned.

Choking

1 If the athlete can cough and speak, encourage coughing and monitor closely. Do not strike the back while the cough is effective.

2 If the athlete cannot speak, breathe or cough effectively, call for emergency help and give 5 firm back blows followed by 5 abdominal thrusts. Repeat until the object clears or the athlete becomes unresponsive.

3 Use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts when the abdomen cannot be encircled or in late pregnancy.

4 If the athlete becomes unresponsive, lower them safely and start CPR with chest compressions. Remove only an object you can clearly see; never perform a blind finger sweep.

Water submersion

1 Do not enter moving or deep water unless trained and equipped. Reach, throw or use a safe flotation aid while another person calls for help.

2 Once safely out of the water, check breathing. If breathing is absent or abnormal, begin CPR with breaths and chest compressions; after drowning, breaths are especially important. Use an AED as soon as available.

3 Do not perform abdominal thrusts or try to drain water from the lungs. Remove wet clothing when practical, insulate and treat hypothermia.

4 Arrange medical assessment after any significant submersion, even if the athlete appears to recover, especially for cough, breathlessness, confusion or persistent fatigue.

Hydration heat and sodium

Heat illness, dehydration and exercise-associated hyponatraemia can overlap. The safest prevention is a tested plan, drinking to thirst and early response to symptoms rather than forced intake.

Hydration that reduces risk

  • Drink in response to thirst. Do not chase a fixed hourly target that makes you drink beyond thirst.
  • Use long training sessions to learn your normal range in different temperatures. A plan is a guide, not a command to keep drinking when bloated or nauseated.
  • Use familiar drinks and foods. Salty food or electrolytes may support a tested plan, but sodium does not make overdrinking safe.
  • Weight gain during an endurance event, a bloated feeling and excessive intake increase concern for hyponatraemia.
  • Never force fluid into a confused, drowsy, vomiting or seizing athlete.

Exertional dehydration

Recognise Thirst, dry mouth, headache, weakness, dizziness, reduced urine and worsening performance with a normal mental state. These signs are not specific, and dehydration can overlap with heat illness or hyponatraemia.

1 Stop or reduce exertion, move to shade, loosen excess clothing and cool the skin if hot.

2 If fully alert, not vomiting and able to swallow, take small repeated sips of a familiar carbohydrate-electrolyte drink guided by thirst. Do not force litres of fluid.

3 If there is confusion, collapse, shock, inability to swallow, repeated vomiting or suspected heatstroke, call for emergency help, cool as indicated and give nothing by mouth.

Heat exhaustion and heatstroke

Heatstroke and hyponatraemia overlap. The diagram shows safe first actions, not a field diagnosis.

Pattern

Recognition

Action

Heat exhaustion

Weakness, thirst, headache, nausea, dizziness, heavy sweating and normal mental state.

Stop, move to shade, loosen clothing, wet and fan the skin, and allow thirst-guided oral fluid if fully alert. If not clearly improving within 15 minutes, call for evacuation.

Heatstroke

Any altered mental state, collapse, seizure, combativeness or marked incoordination in a hot athlete. Skin may be wet or dry.

Emergency. Support airway and breathing, remove excess clothing and cool the whole body immediately. Do not delay cooling to obtain a temperature or transport.

Whole body cooling

1 Move out of direct heat and expose as much skin as practical while protecting privacy.

2 Use cold-water immersion if it can be done safely with continuous airway supervision. Use the coldest available water.

3 If immersion is not possible, repeatedly soak the whole body with cold water and fan vigorously.

4 If only ice packs are available, cover as much skin as possible. With limited packs, cool the cheeks, palms and soles rather than relying only on the neck, armpits and groin.

5 Arrange evacuation while cooling continues. Do not give fever medicines; they do not treat heatstroke.

Exercise associated hyponatraemia

Recognise Headache, nausea, bloating, vomiting, swollen hands or feet, unusual irritability, confusion, staggering, seizure or collapse after prolonged fluid intake. Symptoms may appear during the race or up to 24 hours later.

Act Stop the athlete and contact the medical team. Stop oral fluid when hyponatraemia is suspected. Do not give plain water or sports drink to a confused or vomiting athlete. Severe cases require urgent medical treatment with measured blood sodium and hypertonic saline.

Urine output and urine colour alone cannot reliably distinguish hyponatraemia from dehydration. Give the medical team the athlete's approximate fluid intake, food intake, weight change if known and recent urination history.

Cold exposure and frostbite

Wind, rain, sweat, fatigue and an unexpected stop can produce hypothermia even when the day began warm. Carry insulation and waterproof protection on every route where the compulsory kit requires it.

Recognising hypothermia

Stage

What you may see

What to do

Cold stress

Cold discomfort and strong shivering, but normal thinking and coordination.

Add dry layers, shelter from wind and rain, eat and drink if alert, and reassess before moving.

Mild hypothermia

Persistent shivering, clumsiness, stumbling, slurred speech or slower thinking.

Stop. Shelter, replace wet layers without prolonged exposure, insulate above and below and add safe heat to the trunk.

Moderate or severe

Confusion, drowsiness, inability to walk, weakening or absent shivering, slow breathing or collapse.

Emergency. Handle gently, keep as horizontal as practical, insulate, warm the chest and armpits, monitor breathing and evacuate.

  • Use a vapour barrier or survival blanket outside insulating layers. A thin foil blanket by itself provides little insulation from the ground.
  • Place wrapped heat packs or warm bottles on the chest and armpits. Prevent burns and do not place very hot objects directly on skin.
  • Give a warm sweet drink only when the athlete is fully alert and can swallow. Do not give alcohol.
  • Do not rub limbs, use direct flame, or put a moderately or severely hypothermic athlete into a hot shower or bath.

Frostnip and frostbite

Recognise Numb, pale or waxy skin; loss of dexterity; hard or frozen tissue; or blisters after thawing.

1 Protect the part from pressure and further cold. Remove rings and other constricting items.

2 Do not rub, massage or apply snow, fire, a stove or a hot object.

3 Do not thaw frozen tissue if there is a realistic risk that it will refreeze.

4 When refreezing is impossible and care is more than two hours away, trained responders may rewarm in circulating water at 37 to 39 degrees C. Protect with bulky dry dressings after thawing.

5 Avoid walking on a thawed frostbitten foot unless it is essential for survival. Arrange medical assessment.

Muscle kidney and medication

Severe muscle breakdown can release proteins that damage the kidneys. Early symptoms may be subtle, so treat the combination of severe muscle pain, weakness, swelling and abnormal urine as urgent.

Rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury

Risk rises with Unaccustomed or continuous hard effort, heat illness, dehydration, prior kidney disease and medicines that stress the kidneys, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Recognise Muscle pain or tenderness far beyond expected soreness, unusual weakness, swollen limbs, dark red or cola-coloured urine, or much less urine than usual.

Act Stop running, contact the medical team and arrange urgent assessment. Do not take an NSAID and do not try to solve the problem by forcing litres of water. Medical care may require blood tests and controlled fluid treatment.

Medicines during SkyRun

Medicine or product

Race guidance

Regular prescriptions

Continue exactly as agreed with your clinician. Carry enough for the full event plus a waterproofed spare. Tell the race team about conditions or medicines that affect emergency care.

NSAIDs

Do not use ibuprofen, diclofenac, ketoprofen, naproxen or similar anti-inflammatory medicines before or during the race. They can increase kidney, stomach and bleeding risk.

Paracetamol

Use only within the label and your clinician's advice. Never combine products that contain paracetamol, and do not use it to mask pain that should stop your race.

Aspirin

Do not use aspirin as routine race pain relief. The separate emergency exception for suspected heart attack is described later in this guide.

Anti-nausea and anti-diarrhoeal medicine

Do not borrow or experiment with these during the race. Persistent vomiting, blood, fever or severe localised pain requires medical advice rather than symptom masking.

Stimulants and energy products

Use only a tested caffeine plan. Stimulants cannot correct dangerous sleep loss and may worsen palpitations, anxiety, heat strain or stomach symptoms.

Stomach fuel and cramps

Gastrointestinal symptoms are common in ultra-endurance racing. Most improve when intensity falls, but persistent vomiting and focal pain can signal a condition that should not be run through.

Preventing stomach problems

  • Train with the foods, drinks, concentrations and carrying systems you will use on race day.
  • Keep the final pre-race meals familiar. A lower-fibre or lower-fat choice may help if you have tested it; do not make a last-minute diet change.
  • Start conservatively and use small, frequent amounts of familiar food rather than large concentrated feeds.
  • Adjust early for heat. High intensity and overheating reduce gut tolerance.
  • Do not introduce new gels, salt products, painkillers or stomach medicines during the race.

When nausea vomiting or diarrhoea begins

1 Slow to a walk or stop in a safe place for 15 to 30 minutes.

2 Cool down or add warmth as conditions require. Loosen anything compressing the abdomen.

3 If fully alert, take small sips to thirst. Do not force fluid when bloated or repeatedly vomiting.

4 Restart gently only if symptoms settle, thinking is clear and fluids can be retained.

5 Call the medical team for repeated vomiting, blood, severe or one-sided pain, a rigid abdomen, confusion, collapse or inability to keep fluid down.

Exercise associated muscle cramps

Cramps are most consistently linked to neuromuscular fatigue and individual susceptibility. Salt, magnesium and bananas do not reliably prevent them.

1 Reduce effort and shorten your stride. Stop if your gait becomes unsafe.

2 Gently lengthen the cramped muscle and hold a tolerable stretch. Do not force it.

3 Cool the athlete if hot and drink to thirst using the familiar race plan.

4 Resume only when the cramp has released and normal walking is pain-free.

Persistent one-sided calf pain or swelling, focal bony pain, weakness, dark urine, chest pain or breathlessness is not a routine cramp and needs medical assessment.

Sleep and judgment

Sleep loss reduces coordination, motivation, navigation and risk judgment. Repeated errors and microsleeps are safety signs, not a test of toughness.

Recognising unsafe fatigue

  • Stumbling, weaving or repeated falls.
  • Missing obvious route features or repeating the same navigation error.
  • Microsleeps, head nodding or losing minutes of awareness.
  • Unusual irritability, poor decisions, slowed speech or inability to complete simple tasks.
  • Visual or auditory hallucinations.

A safer response

1 Stop somewhere protected from cliffs, traffic, cold ground, wind and rain.

2 Tell another athlete. Do not continue alone when hallucinating, repeatedly falling or having microsleeps.

3 Add warm layers before resting. Set an alarm and take a short nap of about 10 to 20 minutes.

4 Eat and drink according to your plan after waking, then reassess walking, thinking and navigation.

5 Contact the race team if normal function does not return. Confusion can also indicate heatstroke, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia or head injury.

Before the race

Protect sleep in the weeks before the start, and plan rest around your expected race duration and experience. Caffeine may improve alertness briefly, but it does not replace sleep or make an unsafe athlete safe.

Serious illness and acute distress

New systemic illness is not something to run through. Stop when symptoms affect breathing, circulation, mental state, hydration or safe movement.

Fever or serious infection

Urgent signs Fever with confusion, fainting, severe weakness, stiff neck, a new widespread rash, severe headache, breathing difficulty, repeated vomiting, very low urine, rapidly spreading skin redness or pain out of proportion to the visible injury.

1 Stop the race, rest in shelter and contact the medical team. Keep the athlete comfortably warm or cool without causing shivering.

2 If fully alert and able to swallow, allow small thirst-guided sips. Give nothing by mouth if drowsy, confused, repeatedly vomiting or unable to swallow.

3 Do not use painkillers or fever medicine to mask severe symptoms and continue racing. Arrange urgent evacuation for any change in mental state, shock, breathing difficulty or rapid deterioration.

4 After the race, report recent travel, insect or tick exposure and the event conditions when seeking care for fever, rash or flu-like illness.

Panic or acute distress

Rapid breathing, tingling around the mouth or hands, trembling and intense fear can occur with panic, but heatstroke, asthma, anaphylaxis, low blood sugar, heart problems and other emergencies must be considered first.

1 Move to a safe quiet place, sit with the athlete and speak calmly. Encourage a slow, longer exhalation without forcing deep breaths.

2 Do not use a paper bag. Check for medical red flags and prescribed conditions or medicines.

3 Call for help if symptoms are new, severe, persist beyond a few minutes, or include chest pain, fainting, confusion, blue lips, wheeze or inability to speak normally.

Chest pain stroke and seizures

These conditions are time critical. Stop the race and activate urgent evacuation before trying to establish the exact diagnosis.

Suspected heart attack

Recognise Pressure, tightness or pain in the chest that lasts more than a few minutes or returns; pain in an arm, jaw, back, neck or stomach; unusual shortness of breath; sweating; nausea; pallor or light-headedness. Women, older adults and people with diabetes may have less typical symptoms.

1 Stop all exertion. Keep the athlete still and in the position that is easiest for breathing.

2 Activate urgent evacuation and prepare the AED.

3 While waiting, an alert adult with non-traumatic chest pain may chew 162 to 325 mg aspirin only if there is no aspirin allergy, significant bleeding risk or instruction from a clinician not to take it. If uncertain, wait for medical direction.

4 Monitor breathing and responsiveness. Begin CPR and use the AED if the athlete becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally.

Stroke

Recognise FAST Face droop, Arm weakness, Speech that is slurred or strange, and Time to call immediately. Sudden severe headache, loss of vision, imbalance or one-sided numbness also matter.

1 Note the exact time the athlete was last known to be normal.

2 Activate urgent evacuation. Keep the athlete still, comfortable and warm.

3 Give nothing to eat or drink. Do not give aspirin unless medical control directs it.

4 Monitor the airway and breathing and be ready to start CPR.

Seizure

1 Protect the athlete from rocks, equipment and falls. Cushion the head and time the seizure.

2 Do not restrain the person and put nothing in the mouth.

3 When jerking stops, check breathing and use the recovery position if breathing is normal and injury allows.

4 Call urgently for a first seizure, a seizure longer than 5 minutes, repeated seizures without recovery, pregnancy, diabetes, head injury, breathing difficulty or failure to return to normal awareness within 5 to 10 minutes.

Asthma allergy and low blood sugar

Athletes with a known condition must carry their own prescribed rescue medicine where it can be reached immediately, not buried deep in a pack.

Asthma attack

Recognise Wheeze, cough, chest tightness, breathlessness, increased work of breathing or difficulty speaking in full sentences.

1 Stop and sit upright. Help the athlete use their own prescribed reliever inhaler according to their action plan. A spacer improves delivery when available.

2 Activate urgent evacuation for blue or grey lips, exhaustion, confusion, collapse, very poor air movement, inability to speak, or failure to improve promptly.

3 Monitor continuously and prepare for CPR.

Anaphylaxis

Recognise Rapid onset after a sting, food or medicine, with breathing difficulty, throat or tongue swelling, hoarse voice, widespread hives, vomiting, dizziness or collapse.

1 Use the athlete's prescribed adrenaline auto-injector in the outer mid-thigh immediately and activate urgent evacuation.

2 Lay the athlete flat with legs raised. If breathing is difficult, allow sitting with legs extended; if vomiting, use the recovery position. Do not let the athlete stand or walk.

3 If severe symptoms persist and medical help will take more than 5 to 10 minutes, a second prescribed auto-injector may be used if available.

4 Monitor breathing and responsiveness and be ready for CPR.

Low blood sugar

Recognise Shaking, sweating, hunger, sudden irritability, weakness, confusion, slurred speech, unusual behaviour, collapse or seizure. It is especially important in an athlete using glucose-lowering medicine.

1 If awake and able to swallow, give at least 20 g of fast-acting oral glucose or an equivalent familiar source of simple sugar.

2 If there is no clear improvement within 10 minutes, repeat glucose if the athlete can still swallow and activate medical help.

3 Once recovered, follow with familiar longer-acting carbohydrate and reassess before any movement.

4 If drowsy, unconscious, seizing or unable to swallow, give nothing by mouth. Use the recovery position if breathing normally and evacuate urgently.

High blood sugar and ketoacidosis

Recognise In an athlete with diabetes: unusual thirst and frequent urination, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or fast breathing, fruity-smelling breath, extreme fatigue, confusion or collapse. Illness, missed insulin or pump failure can trigger diabetic ketoacidosis.

1 Stop exertion and contact the medical team. Check glucose and ketones only with the athlete's own equipment and follow their written diabetes plan.

2 Do not try to lower high glucose with exercise, do not give someone else's insulin and do not improvise an extra dose.

3 Repeated vomiting, deep or difficult breathing, moderate or high ketones, increasing drowsiness, confusion or inability to drink is an emergency requiring urgent evacuation.

Bleeding wounds and shock

Severe bleeding can kill within minutes. Direct pressure is the first action; a tourniquet is for life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure cannot control or when pressure is not practical.

Life threatening bleeding

1 Expose the wound and apply firm continuous direct pressure with gauze or clean cloth. Do not keep lifting the dressing to check.

2 If blood soaks through, keep pressure on and add more material. Pack a deep wound with gauze only if trained.

3 For uncontrolled life-threatening limb bleeding, apply a commercial tourniquet above the wound and not over a joint. Tighten until bleeding stops, note the time, and do not loosen it in the field.

4 A commercial tourniquet is preferred. If an improvised tourniquet is unavoidable, it must be a broad band with a rigid windlass; narrow cords cause harm and often fail.

5 Treat for shock, protect from the ground and weather, and evacuate urgently.

Shock

Recognise Pale or clammy skin, fast breathing, weakness, thirst, restlessness, confusion, faintness or collapse after bleeding, trauma, allergy or severe illness.

  • Control the cause when possible and activate evacuation.
  • Lay the athlete flat if breathing allows and no injury makes that position unsafe.
  • Keep warm without overheating and monitor breathing and mental state.
  • Do not give food or drink to a severely injured, confused or vomiting athlete.

Nosebleed

1 Sit the athlete leaning forward with the mouth open. Pinch the soft part of the nose continuously for 10 to 15 minutes.

2 Spit out blood that enters the mouth. Do not tilt the head back, lie flat or pack material deep into the nose.

3 Seek urgent care for heavy bleeding, breathing difficulty, significant facial or head trauma, faintness, anticoagulant use, or bleeding that continues after 20 minutes of firm pressure.

Impaled object

  • Do not remove the object.
  • Control bleeding around it and stabilise it with bulky padding without pressing it deeper.
  • Immobilise the area and evacuate. A penetrating eye injury is covered later in this guide.

Head neck bone and joint injury

A remote evacuation becomes harder after repeated attempts to walk on an unstable injury. Stop early when weight bearing, balance, circulation or sensation is abnormal.

Head and neck injury

Urgent signs Loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, seizure, increasing drowsiness, unequal pupils, weakness, confusion, unusual behaviour, fluid from the nose or ears, neck pain, or numbness and tingling.

1 Stop the race and activate evacuation. An athlete with suspected concussion does not return to the event that day.

2 Keep the head and neck in a comfortable neutral position and discourage unnecessary movement. Maintain the airway and breathing.

3 Move the athlete only for immediate danger, airway care or essential evacuation. Protect from cold and reassess often.

Fracture and dislocation

  • Suspect fracture with deformity, focal bony tenderness, severe swelling, an open wound over bone, or inability to take four steps.
  • Check circulation, sensation and movement beyond the injury before and after splinting.
  • Cover an open fracture with a clean dressing. Do not push exposed bone back.
  • Splint the limb in the position found, with padding and support above and below the injury. Do not relocate a dislocation.
  • Blue, pale or cold skin, a weak or absent pulse, loss of feeling, or increasing severe pain is limb threatening and requires urgent evacuation.

Sprain strain and overuse pain

1 Stop and assess. Protect the injury and use support or a compression bandage that does not cause numbness or colour change.

2 Cool briefly for pain if useful, with material between cold source and skin. Elevate when resting.

3 Do not repeatedly test a painful joint. Do not continue with instability, locking, focal bone pain, severe swelling, altered sensation or an abnormal gait.

4 Diffuse shin pain may be overuse; sharp focal pain, pain at rest or night pain raises concern for a stress fracture.

Chest abdominal and pelvic injury

A hard fall, rock strike or penetrating wound can cause dangerous internal injury without dramatic external bleeding. Treat breathing difficulty, shock or worsening torso pain as urgent.

Serious torso trauma

Urgent signs Breathing difficulty after trauma, unequal chest movement, coughing blood, an open chest wound, severe chest or abdominal pain, a rigid or swollen abdomen, pelvic pain, blood in urine, pallor, faintness or confusion.

1 Activate urgent evacuation. Keep the athlete still in the position found unless the scene is unsafe or airway care requires movement. Support the head and neck after a significant fall.

2 For breathing difficulty, allow the most comfortable position if there is no suspected spine, hip or pelvic injury and the athlete is not in shock. Monitor continuously.

3 For an open chest wound, leave it exposed, cover with a clean non-occlusive dry dressing, or use a vented chest seal only if trained. If breathing worsens after any dressing is applied, loosen or remove it.

4 For suspected abdominal or pelvic injury, do not press repeatedly on the area, do not bind the pelvis unless specifically trained, and give nothing to eat or drink.

5 Treat for shock, keep warm without overheating and prepare for CPR. Do not remove an impaled object.

Snakebite eye injury and lightning

These incidents demand calm, minimum movement and early communication. Do not spend time on unproven treatments or trying to capture wildlife.

Snakebite

1 Move away from the snake. Do not try to catch or kill it. A photo from a safe distance may help only if it causes no delay or risk.

2 Lay the person down, reassure them and keep the whole body still. Remove rings, watches and tight footwear before swelling develops.

3 Splint the bitten limb in a comfortable position and carry the person whenever possible. Mark the edge of swelling and the time without delaying evacuation.

4 Do not cut, suck, ice, massage, apply chemicals, or use a tight arterial tourniquet.

5 Pressure immobilisation requires specific training and is only appropriate for some neurotoxic bites without local swelling. Do not improvise it unless the race medical team directs you.

6 Arrange urgent evacuation and monitor airway and breathing. If vomiting and breathing normally, use the left-side recovery position.

Eye injury

  • Loose grit or irritant: prevent rubbing and flush gently with clean water or saline from the inner to outer corner.
  • Chemical exposure: begin continuous irrigation immediately for at least 20 minutes, remove contact lenses if easy, and seek medical assessment.
  • Blunt injury: use a cool compress around the eye without pressure. Double vision, visual loss, severe pain or a misshapen pupil requires urgent care.
  • Penetrating object: do not remove it or press the eye. Stabilise with a rigid shield, limit eye movement and evacuate urgently.

Lightning

  • At the first thunder, leave ridges, peaks and exposed high ground and follow race instructions. A substantial building or enclosed hard-top vehicle is the safest shelter when available.
  • If no safe shelter exists, move away from isolated trees, rock overhangs, water, wire fences and other conductors. Separate the group to reduce multiple casualties.
  • A lightning casualty does not retain electrical charge and is safe to touch. Call for help, check breathing and begin CPR and AED use when indicated.

Altitude illness

  • On routes above about 2500 m, new headache with nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue or poor coordination may be acute altitude illness.
  • Stop ascending. Do not continue higher with symptoms, and descend if they worsen or do not settle.
  • Confusion, inability to walk a straight line, breathlessness at rest, cough or chest tightness is an emergency. Descend, contact the race team and do not leave the athlete alone.

Bites stings and toxic exposure

Protect yourself first, move away from the hazard and watch for breathing difficulty, collapse, severe spreading pain or whole-body symptoms.

Bites stings and ticks

Bee or wasp sting Move away from the insects, remove any visible stinger promptly by scraping or plucking, wash the area and use a wrapped cold pack for local pain. Use the anaphylaxis response for breathing difficulty, facial or tongue swelling, widespread hives, vomiting, dizziness or collapse. Multiple stings or a sting to the eye or mouth needs urgent assessment.

Spider or scorpion Wash the site and use a wrapped cold pack. Do not cut, suck, apply chemicals or use a tourniquet. Seek urgent care for severe or spreading pain, muscle cramping, weakness, sweating, vomiting, an open wound, dark urine, breathing difficulty or symptoms beyond the bite or sting site.

Tick Use fine tweezers to grasp the tick close to the skin and pull upward with steady even pressure. Do not burn, twist or smother it. Wash the site and hands, record the date and seek care for a spreading rash, fever, severe headache or flu-like illness.

Animal or human bite

1 Get away from the animal without attempting to catch or kill it. Control serious bleeding first.

2 Wash and flush every bite or scratch immediately with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes, then cover loosely with a clean dressing.

3 Arrange same-day medical assessment for wound care, tetanus review, antibiotics when indicated and urgent rabies post-exposure treatment. This applies to bites, scratches and saliva on broken skin or mucous membranes from a potentially rabid mammal.

4 Do not delay care while trying to determine whether the animal looked healthy or was vaccinated.

Poison smoke and chemical exposure

1 Do not enter an unsafe area or expose yourself to fumes, fire, fuel, pesticides or an unknown substance. Move the athlete to fresh air only when it is safe and call Jazzy or ACE Air and Ambulance.

2 Suspect carbon monoxide when people near a stove, fire, generator or vehicle develop headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness or confusion. Move everyone into fresh air, do not re-enter the space and arrange urgent assessment.

3 For skin chemicals, put on gloves, brush off dry material before adding water, remove contaminated clothing and irrigate with running water for at least 15 minutes. Irrigate chemical eye exposure continuously for at least 20 minutes.

4 For swallowed poison, an unknown medicine or suspected overdose, do not induce vomiting and give nothing by mouth unless medical control directs it. Keep the container, label or a photograph for responders.

5 If the athlete is unresponsive and not breathing normally, begin CPR with breaths. If opioid overdose is suspected and naloxone is available, a trained responder may give it without delaying CPR or evacuation.

Foot and skin care

Treat friction and moisture early. A small hot spot is easier to manage than a blister that changes your gait and creates a second injury.

Hot spots and blisters

1 Stop as soon as you feel burning or rubbing. Clean and dry the area and correct the cause by adjusting the shoe, sock, insole or lacing.

2 Protect intact skin with a familiar lubricant, tape or blister dressing. Do not tape over dirt, grit or wet folds.

3 Leave an intact blister roof in place. If drainage is essential for safe movement, use clean equipment and technique only if trained, drain from the edge and preserve the roof.

4 Cover with a clean dressing and reassess. Spreading redness, warmth, pus, red streaks, fever or severe pain needs medical care.

Maceration chafing and sun

  • Change wet socks when possible, air feet briefly in a safe place and use dry foot powder or barrier product that you tested in training.
  • Clean chafed skin gently, dry it, protect with a familiar barrier and change the clothing or pack feature causing friction.
  • Use sunscreen, lip protection, a hat and eye protection appropriate to the conditions. Reapply sunscreen according to the product instructions.
  • Seek help for extensive skin loss, infection, severe swelling, inability to bear weight or a change in gait that cannot be corrected.

Cuts grazes and burns

  • Rinse cuts and grazes with clean water, remove visible grit if easy, pat dry and cover with a clean dressing. Control bleeding first when needed.
  • Cool a thermal burn promptly with clean cool running water for up to 20 minutes. Do not apply ice, butter, cream or adhesive directly to the burn.
  • Cover loosely with a clean non-stick dressing. Seek urgent care for deep or extensive burns, burns to the face, hands, feet or genitals, electrical burns, or any breathing difficulty after smoke or heat exposure.

Before race preparation

The safest field treatment begins before race day. Plan for your own conditions, the event's compulsory kit and the possibility of a long wait for evacuation.

Medical readiness

  • Discuss the event with your clinician if you have heart, lung, kidney, endocrine or neurological disease; a history of heat illness or severe allergy; recent surgery or infection; or unexplained chest pain, fainting or breathlessness.
  • Submit accurate medical information and tell the race team about allergies, important medicines and emergency devices.
  • Carry a waterproof medical card with your name, race number, emergency contact, conditions, allergies and medicines.
  • Know how your medicines interact with heat, cold, altitude, prolonged exercise and missed meals.
  • Check that your medical, rescue, evacuation and repatriation cover applies to competitive remote endurance events in the country where you race.

After race warning signs

Some complications develop after the finish. Do not drive yourself or go to sleep alone when you are confused, faint, severely unwell or unable to keep fluids down.

Seek urgent medical care

  • Chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, palpitations with dizziness, stroke signs or a seizure.
  • Confusion, worsening headache, repeated vomiting, agitation, extreme drowsiness or new imbalance.
  • Dark red or cola urine, very low urine output, severe muscle pain, weakness or swelling.
  • Persistent vomiting, severe or localised abdominal pain, blood in vomit or stool, or inability to drink.
  • Fever or worsening illness after heatstroke, even if cooling made you feel better initially.
  • Increasing limb pain or swelling, especially one-sided calf swelling with chest pain or breathlessness; numbness, colour change, an infected wound, or inability to bear weight.
  • New cough, breathing difficulty, fever or worsening fatigue after water submersion, smoke exposure or carbon monoxide concern.
  • Fever, rash, severe headache or flu-like illness after a tick, animal bite, insect exposure or travel; seek immediate rabies assessment after any relevant mammal bite or scratch.

Recovery decisions

Use thirst, appetite and symptoms to guide ordinary recovery. Do not force large volumes of water. Continue to watch urine, mental state, breathing and muscle pain for at least 24 hours after a long event, because hyponatraemia and kidney injury can appear after exercise.

Any athlete treated for heatstroke, significant hypothermia, anaphylaxis, chest pain, seizure, head injury, snakebite, severe bleeding or suspected rhabdomyolysis needs professional follow-up and medical clearance before returning to training.
